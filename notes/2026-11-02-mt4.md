Type: research

## Who needs this, and can it work?

What I did: read papers on (1) how people with hearing loss experience music, and (2) whether you can tell from EEG which instrument someone is listening to.

What I found:
- Hearing aids are bad for music. In a survey of 176 hearing aid users, 67% had some trouble with music [Greasley2020].
- Cochlear implant users liked music better when the vocals were turned up by about 8 dB. People with normal hearing preferred the original [Tahmasebi2020].
- Telling which instrument someone is listening to from EEG works, but not very well: about 71% with a full lab cap over 60 s [Simon2023], and about 60% for implant users [Althoff2026].
- A Sony study got about 86% using a cheap 4-electrode headband (Muse 2), but only tested 8 people [Akama2026].
- Brain-reading models trained on speech don't work on music [Simon2023].
- Headsets with electrodes only around the ear do much worse than a full cap [Zhu2024]. Real-time systems take 5–12 s to notice when you switch attention [COCOHA2024].

What I learned:
- The users are real: people with hearing loss who struggle with music.
- A simple fixed setting ("vocals always louder") already helps them, so our product has to beat that.
- There is very little EEG data recorded during music, which is a reason to generate fake data (my area).

## Rethinking what the product is for

What I did: questioned whether the MT3 idea made sense, and who it is actually for.

The problem with the MT3 pitch:
- For someone with normal hearing, a volume slider does the job better than EEG. It's faster, cheaper and more reliable.
- We listed too many possible users.

New version:
- Users: people with hearing aids or cochlear implants, who find music muddy.
- Why turning it all up doesn't help: hearing loss blurs sounds together, and louder doesn't separate them.
- Product: a music player that slowly turns up the part you're following and turns the rest down a little. It only changes when it's confident, and otherwise stays on your normal setting.
- Why EEG and not a button: it keeps up as your attention moves during a song, and works when your hands or eyes are busy.

What changes for testing: compare against a fixed setting like "vocals louder", not just against the original song.

## Planning the EEG simulator

What I did: worked out what the simulator should produce, checked whether something like it already exists, and found a dataset to build it from.

What it makes: fake EEG of someone listening to a song while focusing on one instrument, as it would look on a given headset. It's built in steps:
1. How loud each instrument is over time.
2. How the brain responds to that loudness, learned from real recordings.
3. A boost for the instrument being listened to.
4. Background brain activity, taken from real recordings.
5. Noise like blinks, and the electrode positions of a chosen headset.

What it's for: training data (real music EEG is scarce), and predicting how well a cheap headset would work before buying one.

Does it already exist? Tools exist for parts of it: simulating EEG in general [Gramfort2013], background brain activity [Cole2019], fitting brain responses [Brodbeck2023], and simulating EEG for short events in MATLAB [Krol2018]. One group generated fake EEG with AI for speech, and it barely helped [Rannaleet2026]. I found nothing that turns music into labelled fake EEG for a given headset. So what's new is the combination and the experiments, not the individual parts.

Dataset: MAD-EEG [Cantisani2019]. Downloaded it. It has 6 usable people and no electrodes near the ears, so it can't directly tell us about ear-based headsets.

Plan for MT5: check the dataset shows a clear brain response to the music; get a baseline accuracy on real data with Cody; build a first simple version of the simulator; get the headset shortlist from the team.

## Setting up the repo and data

What I did:
- Created the team GitHub repo: https://github.com/b-adamson/attention-earbuds. Kept it minimal (README, data notes) so the team can agree the structure together.
- Downloaded MAD-EEG [Cantisani2019] and added a small loader (simulation/load_madeeg.py) that reads one trial into a Python dictionary.

What the data looks like:
- Stored as HDF5: one file organised like folders. Subject → trial → arrays. A YAML file describes each trial (instruments, which one was attended, sample rates).
- Each trial has the EEG (20 electrodes, 256 Hz), the stereo mix heard, and each instrument's track on its own (44.1 kHz). Duos have an empty third instrument slot.
- 6 subjects and 246 trials in the preprocessed file. No electrodes near the ears.
